Pokémon card scanner 2026 Take a photo of a card, get the exact card and its price.

You point your phone at a card and it tells you which of about 20,000 cards it is, plus what it's worth. It compares the photo against every card image (CLIP embeddings and a FAISS index), then reads the collector number in the corner with OCR to double check.

The hard part wasn't recognizing the card, it was finding it in the photo. On 99 real scans it was never wrong when it gave an answer, but it only found the card about 30% of the time, because light borders on light tables broke the edge detection. I ended up running several detection methods on each photo and keeping whichever crop matched a card best. That got it to 63% without breaking anything that already worked.

Python, FastAPI, OpenCV, CLIP, FAISS, React

HexaFlow 2026 NASA HUNCH payload that waters plants in microgravity.

HUNCH is a NASA program where high school students build real hardware for space. Our design is a hexagonal dome with grooves that move water to a plant using capillary action, so it works without gravity. NASA drop tested it with us on August 6, 2026.

I led the website, including a 3D viewer where you can pull the assembly apart and see each part.

Astro, Tailwind, Three.js

Code on GitHub

Jarvis 2026 A Raspberry Pi system on my wall that plans my day.

A vertical monitor and a touchscreen in my room, plus a phone version I can open anywhere. It shows my goals, schedule, deadlines and project status, and asks me about things I've been ignoring.

I wanted it to cost nothing to run, so the AI parts use local models (Ollama) on my desktop instead of paid APIs, and the always-on parts run on Raspberry Pi 5s. The phone connects over Tailscale, so nothing is open to the internet. I use it every day.

Python, FastAPI, Svelte, Raspberry Pi, Ollama, Tailscale

Forex signal research 2026 I built a trading signal app, then proved it didn't work.

A desktop app that scanned currency charts and called short-term moves. Before trusting it, I backtested it on data it had never seen. It was right about 46% of the time, and it needed 55.6% just to break even.

I trained real ML models to check whether the problem was my scoring. They got about 51%, so the signal just isn't there. I turned the app into a research and journal tool instead. Probably the most useful thing I learned all year: test it before you believe it.

JavaScript, Electron, Node, backtesting
