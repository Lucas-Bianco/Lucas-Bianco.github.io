This is the project where I learned the most by being wrong.

I built a desktop app (Electron, with a Node engine) that scanned forex and metals charts and gave short-term "up or down" calls, the kind of thing binary options platforms sell. It pulled real price data through a chain of providers, so if one data source failed it fell back to the next. It scored setups with a weighted set of technical indicators. Despite the "AI" in the original name, the scoring was hand-tuned rules, not machine learning.

It never placed a trade. That was a rule from the start. Before anything touched money I wanted to know if the signals actually worked.

## The test

The engine already had a backtester, walk-forward testing (train on one stretch of history, test on the next stretch it has never seen), confidence intervals and calibration checks. I wrote more tools on top of that to answer one question honestly: do these calls win often enough to make money?

Binary options make the math simple and brutal. With an 80% payout, you win $0.80 when you're right and lose $1 when you're wrong. To break even you need to be right **55.6%** of the time.

Pooled across everything, out of sample, the signals won **46%** of the time. Worse than a coin flip, and nowhere near 55.6%.

## Trying to save it

I didn't want to give up after one test, so I tried the obvious fixes:

- **Only trade when higher timeframes agree, only take high-confidence calls, stick to trending setups.** That lifted it to about break-even, around 56%, but not by enough to be statistically above break-even. Filtering harder mostly just meant fewer trades.
- **Swap the hand-tuned rules for real machine learning.** I trained logistic regression and gradient boosting models to predict direction. Both landed around **51%** out of sample.

That second result was the important one. If a properly trained model can't beat a coin flip either, the problem isn't my indicator weights. There's just no short-horizon signal there to find. Major currency pairs are some of the most heavily traded markets in the world, and short-term direction is about as close to random as it gets.

## Looking for any edge at all

So I widened the search. Ten years of daily data on 8 instruments, everything measured after trading costs, with walk-forward splits and a gap between training and testing so nothing leaked. I wrote 23 validation checks for the testing tool itself, including one that makes sure my trend-strength indicator matched the engine's exact numbers.

- **Simple trend following on the major pairs** was about Sharpe 0 after costs. (Sharpe is return divided by risk. Above 1 is good.)
- **Ranking pairs against each other by momentum** lost money out of sample at every setting.
- **Carry** (earning the interest rate gap) looked fine in training at Sharpe 0.56, then went to -0.05 out of sample.
- **Long-horizon trend, only when the trend is strong** (a 200-day lookback, filtered by an ADX trend-strength reading of 20 or more) did best: **out-of-sample Sharpe about 1.05**, with a max drawdown of 3.3%.

That last one looked great until I checked two things. First, its t-statistic was **1.74**, and you want at least 2 before you call something real. Second, when I removed gold and re-ran it on currencies only, it fell apart. Gold was doing almost all the work. So the most honest summary is: there might be a small long-term trend edge in gold, and I haven't proven it.

## Why binaries can't work for this

The bigger lesson was about the shape of the bet. Trend following works because of an uneven payoff. You lose small amounts often, and once in a while you catch a big move that pays for all of it. The win rate is usually below 50%, and that's fine.

A binary option pays the same fixed amount whether the price moves a little or a lot. It cuts off the big wins that trend following depends on. So even the one idea that showed promise in my tests can't work as a binary bet. It only makes sense with a normal position where a big move can pay off big.

## What I took from it

- **Test before you believe.** The app looked convincing. The signals felt right on the chart. The numbers said otherwise, and the numbers win.
- **Out of sample is the only number that counts.** Carry looked good in training and died on new data.
- **When a real ML model can't beat a coin flip, stop tuning.** It's a signal problem, not a model problem.
- **Check what's doing the work.** The "gold trend" result was really just gold.

I stopped treating it as a signal app and turned it into a research and journal tool: log ideas, forward-test them on live data without money, and track whether they hold up. The same instincts went straight into my [Pokémon card scanner](/projects/pokemon-card-scanner/), which is a much messier market where an edge can actually exist.
