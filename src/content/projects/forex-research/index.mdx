This is the project where I learned the most by being wrong.

## The problem

Binary options platforms sell short-term "up or down" calls on currencies and metals. I wanted to know if I could build something that made those calls well enough to win.

The math is simple and brutal. With an **80% payout**, you win $0.80 when you're right and lose $1 when you're wrong. To break even you need to be right **55.6%** of the time.

## The solution

I built a desktop app in **Electron** with a **Node.js** engine that scanned forex and metals charts and gave short-term calls.

- It pulled real price data through a **chain of data providers**, so if one failed it fell back to the next.
- It scored setups with a **weighted set of technical indicators**. Despite "AI" in the original name, those were hand-tuned rules, not machine learning.
- It **never placed a trade**. That was a rule from the start: before anything touched money, I wanted to know if the signals worked.

## How it works

The engine already had a **backtester**, **walk-forward testing** (train on one stretch of history, test on the next stretch it has never seen), confidence intervals and calibration checks. I wrote more tools on top to answer one question: do these calls win often enough to make money?

For the wider search later on, I used ten years of daily data on 8 instruments, measured everything **after trading costs**, and put a gap between training and test windows so nothing leaked. I wrote **23 validation checks** for the testing tool itself, including one that made sure my trend-strength indicator matched the engine's numbers exactly.

## Testing

### The short-term signals

Pooled across everything, out of sample, the signals won **46%** of the time. Worse than a coin flip, and nowhere near 55.6%.

I tried the obvious fixes before giving up:

- **Filter harder**: only trade when higher timeframes agree, only high-confidence calls, only trending setups. That got it to around break-even (about 56%), but not by enough to be statistically above it. Filtering mostly just meant fewer trades.
- **Replace the rules with real ML**: I trained **logistic regression** and **gradient boosting** models to predict direction. Both landed around **51%** out of sample.

That second result was the important one. If a properly trained model can't beat a coin flip either, my indicator weights aren't the problem. There's no short-horizon signal there to find. Major currency pairs are some of the most heavily traded markets in the world.

### Looking for any edge at all

| Strategy | Out-of-sample result |
|---|---|
| Simple trend following, major pairs | Sharpe about 0 after costs |
| Ranking pairs by momentum | Lost money at every setting |
| Carry (earning the interest rate gap) | Sharpe 0.56 in training, -0.05 out of sample |
| **200-day trend, only when ADX is 20 or more** | **Sharpe about 1.05**, max drawdown 3.3% |

Sharpe is return divided by risk. Above 1 is good.

That last row looked great until I checked two things. Its t-statistic was **1.74**, and you want at least 2 before calling something real. And when I removed gold and re-ran it on currencies only, it fell apart. Gold was doing almost all the work.

## Results

- The short-term signals **don't work**: 46% against 55.6% needed.
- Real ML models couldn't do better than about 51%, which points at the market, not the model.
- There **might** be a small long-term trend edge in gold. I haven't proven it.

The bigger finding was about the shape of the bet. Trend following works because of an uneven payoff: you lose small amounts often, and now and then catch a big move that pays for everything. The win rate is usually below 50%, and that's fine.

A binary option pays the same fixed amount whether the price moves a little or a lot. It cuts off the big wins trend following depends on. So even the one promising idea can't work as a binary bet. It only makes sense with a normal position, where a big move can pay big.

## What I learned

- **Test before you believe.** The signals felt right on the chart. The numbers said otherwise, and the numbers win.
- **Out of sample is the only number that counts.** Carry looked good in training and died on new data.
- **When a real ML model can't beat a coin flip, stop tuning.** It's a signal problem.
- **Check what's doing the work.** The "gold trend" result was really just gold.

## What's next

I stopped treating it as a signal app and turned it into a **research and journal tool**: log ideas, forward-test them on live data without money, and track whether they hold up. The same habits went straight into my [Pokémon card scanner](/projects/pokemon-card-scanner/), a much messier market where an edge can actually exist.
